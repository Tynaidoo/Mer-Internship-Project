Module 3 • Session 2

Digital Literacy & Information Literacy

Learn to evaluate digital information, distinguish evidence from claims, and verify information before acting on it or passing it to others.

Digital Literacy • Source Evaluation • AI Hallucinations • Verification

Purpose

To enable interns to evaluate digital information, distinguish evidence from claims, and verify information before acting on it or passing it to others in an ecosystem flooded with AI content.
Time allocation: 45–60 minutes

Learning outcomes

By the end of the session, interns should be able to:

  • Distinguish facts, opinions, claims, and misinformation/disinformation.
  • Differentiate primary and secondary sources.
  • Evaluate source credibility, purpose, evidence, and currency.
  • Recognise that search ranking and SEO are not proof of reliability.
  • Identify AI hallucinations and apply cross-checking and lateral verification techniques.
Spark

When you Google a question and see an answer displayed at the top of the search page or generated by AI, how do you verify whether it is actually accurate?

Source Taxonomy, Evidence vs. Search Results & Bias

Information abundance has created a digital verification crisis.

Primary Sources

Raw data, direct research studies, original legal documents and financial reports.

Secondary Sources

News articles, blog reviews, market summaries and textbook analyses.

Search Engine Limits

High search rankings or Search Engine Optimisation (SEO) do not guarantee truth; they prioritise relevance algorithms and commercial engagement.

Facts, Opinions, and Bias

A fact is testable against evidence, whereas an opinion expresses judgement or preference. Misinformation is false information shared without intent to deceive; disinformation is deliberate deception. Bias arises from selection, framing, commercial incentives, or assumptions.

AI Hallucinations & Verification Protocols

AI operates probabilistically, predicting likely outputs rather than functioning as a live truth database. This can result in several types of error and risk.

RiskWhat it meansExample
HallucinationAI produces information that sounds credible but is incorrect or fabricated.Inventing a statistic or academic reference.
BiasAI output may reproduce or amplify biases in data or systems.Producing systematically different recommendations for different groups.
PrivacySensitive information may be exposed or handled inappropriately.Uploading confidential customer information to an unapproved AI tool.
SecurityAI systems can introduce or amplify cybersecurity risks.AI-generated code containing vulnerabilities.
Copyright / IPAI-generated or AI-assisted work can raise intellectual-property questions.Using generated material without considering ownership or licensing requirements.
Over-reliancePeople may accept AI output without checking it.Sending an AI-generated report directly to a client.
Automation biasPeople may assume the computer is correct simply because it produced an answer.Accepting an AI recommendation despite contradictory evidence.
MisinformationAI makes it easier and cheaper to generate convincing false content.Generating fabricated news or misleading images.
Lack of transparencyIt may be difficult to understand why an AI system produced a particular result.A recruitment system rejects a candidate without an understandable explanation.

Verification Guide

1. TraceFind the original source behind a claim.
2. Cross-checkCompare the claim with independent, credible sources.
3. InspectCheck author, evidence, date, purpose and possible bias.
4. VerifyConfirm citations, statistics and key facts before professional use.
