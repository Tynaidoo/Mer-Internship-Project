Module 2 • Session 5

Self-Awareness & Emotional Intelligence

Recognise emotions, strengths, behavioural patterns and triggers while developing empathy, workplace self-management and careful interpretation of social cues.

Self-Awareness • EQ • Empathy • Emotional Regulation

Purpose

Help interns recognise their strengths, emotions, behavioural patterns, and triggers, while developing empathy, workplace self-management, and social cue comprehension.
Time allocation: 45–60 minutes

Learning outcomes

Interns should be able to:

  • Describe self-awareness and emotional intelligence in practical workplace terms using EQ models.
  • Identify personal emotional triggers, strengths, and development areas using evidence and feedback.
  • Use practical regulation strategies to manage emotional responses at work and evaluate their ripple effects.
  • Interpret verbal and non-verbal social cues cautiously, avoiding assumptions.
Spark

Have you ever worked with someone who was brilliant technically but difficult to work with due to lack of emotional self-awareness? How did it affect the team?

Emotional Intelligence

Emotional Intelligence (EQ), popularised by Daniel Goleman, comprises four core domains:

🪞 Self-Awareness

Recognising your emotions, strengths, limitations, and triggers as they happen.

🧘 Self-Management

Controlling impulsive reactions and maintaining emotional balance under pressure.

🤝 Social Awareness (Empathy)

Understanding others' perspectives and reading organisational dynamics.

💬 Relationship Management

Inspiring, influencing, and connecting with colleagues.

Activity — Emotional Intelligence in the Workplace

Read each workplace scenario and select the answer that best demonstrates emotional intelligence. Consider which of the four EQ domains is being demonstrated.

Question 1

Your supervisor gives you feedback that you initially find frustrating. You notice that you are becoming defensive and irritated. What does recognising your emotional reaction demonstrate?

Question 2

You receive an email criticising your work. You feel angry and immediately want to respond. Instead, you take a few minutes to calm down before replying professionally. Which EQ domain are you demonstrating?

Question 3

A colleague has been unusually quiet during team meetings. Instead of assuming they are uninterested, you consider that they may be dealing with a difficult situation and check in with them privately. Which EQ domain does this demonstrate?

Question 4

You realise that you tend to become stressed when several tasks have competing deadlines. You identify this as one of your personal triggers and start planning your workload earlier. Which EQ domain is primarily demonstrated?

Question 5

During a disagreement with a colleague, you strongly disagree with their opinion. You remain calm, listen to their concerns and avoid interrupting them. Which EQ domain is most clearly demonstrated?

Question 6

You notice that a new team member rarely contributes during meetings. You consider the possibility that they may be uncomfortable speaking in a new environment and deliberately create an opportunity for them to share their ideas. Which EQ domain does this demonstrate?

Question 7

Two colleagues are having difficulty working together. You listen to both perspectives, help them identify common ground and encourage them to agree on a way forward. Which EQ domain is most relevant?

Question 8

You know that one of your strengths is staying organised, but you also recognise that you sometimes struggle to ask for help because you want to solve everything yourself. What does this demonstrate?

Question 9

Your team is under significant pressure because an important deadline has been moved forward. You feel anxious, but you remain focused, prioritise your tasks and communicate calmly with your colleagues. Which EQ domain is primarily demonstrated?

Question 10

You want your team to adopt a new way of working. Instead of simply telling everyone what to do, you explain the benefits, listen to concerns, involve colleagues in the process and encourage them to support the change. Which EQ domain is primarily demonstrated?

Social Cues, Empathy & Emotional Regulation

Workplace interactions extend beyond verbal communication, especially in hybrid settings. Below are some tips for decoding non-verbal cues, being aware of your emotions, and practising empathy.

  • Decoding Non-Verbal Cues: Body language, tone, facial expression, crossed arms, rushed voice, silence, or delayed chat responses are cues, but they are highly ambiguous. Avoid assuming you know what someone feels; check and listen.
  • The Behavioural Ripple Effect: Your attitude is contagious. A panicked response creates team anxiety; a calm, solution-oriented response stabilises the team. Empathy means asking, “What pressure might my manager or colleague be under right now?”

ConceptExplanation
🪞 Self-AwarenessSelf-awareness involves noticing your emotions, assumptions, strengths, limitations and patterns. Emotional intelligence is often discussed through recognising emotions, managing responses, understanding others and navigating relationships. The goal is not to suppress emotion; it is to notice what is happening and choose an effective response.
🤝 EmpathyEmpathy means trying to understand another person's perspective and experience; it does not require agreement. Social cues include tone, pace, facial expression, silence and context, but they are ambiguous. Avoid assuming you know what someone feels from one cue. Ask, check, and listen.
🧘 Managing Emotions at WorkUse the Pause-Name-Choose Micro-Skill. Regulation can include breathing, taking a short break where appropriate, writing before sending a message, or asking for time to think.
1. PAUSEWhat will you do before responding?
2. NAMEWhat emotion are you experiencing and what triggered it?
3. CHOOSEWhat response would be professional and constructive?

Activity: Managing Emotions — Charged Message Challenge

Instructions: Choose one message. Draft your immediate internal reaction, then Pause, Name, Choose and write the professional response you would actually send.

Message 1 — Critical Feedback

“I’m honestly disappointed with the quality of this work. We’ve already discussed these issues before, and I shouldn’t have to keep pointing them out. Please fix it before sending anything else to me.”

Message 2 — Blame

“This delay has put the entire team under pressure. I was expecting you to have this completed by now. You need to take more responsibility for your deadlines.”

Message 3 — Dismissive Tone

“I don’t think you’re understanding what I’ve asked for. I’ve explained this several times already. Please go back and read the instructions properly before coming back to me.”
